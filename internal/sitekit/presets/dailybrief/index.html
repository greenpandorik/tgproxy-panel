Semiconductors

The quiet scramble to build the chips nobody photographs

Analogue foundries in Dresden, Albany and Hsinchu are unfashionable, badly understood and suddenly the tightest link in the chain.

By Amara Whitfield, technology editor

Nothing about the Dresden plant suggests scarcity. It sits behind a supermarket car park, four storeys of grey cladding, and it makes parts that cost eleven cents each. Yet three carmakers have sent staff to sit in its lobby this quarter, because the eleven-cent part is the one they cannot get.

The industry calls these analogue and power devices. They convert voltages, read sensors and keep motors turning. They are built on process nodes that were considered old in 2009, and for fifteen years almost nobody invested in more of them. Demand did not behave: an electric hatchback carries roughly nine times as many of these devices as the petrol model it replaced.

Capacity is now being added, but slowly and in awkward places. Infineon and onsemi have both committed to expansions that will not produce revenue until 2028. A smaller German group, Silvanta, has taken the stranger route of buying two decommissioned 200mm lines outright and rebuilding them, which its chief executive Ruth Kleiner describes as "shopping in a scrapyard with a very good list".

The interesting question is what happens if the shortage clears. Analogue margins have been unusually good for four years, and a great deal of the capital arriving now is priced on the assumption that they stay there. Two industry forecasters we spoke to think the surplus arrives in late 2027. Neither would put a name to it.

Sterling holds its nerve as the Bank waits one more month

The Monetary Policy Committee split seven to two, with both dissenters arguing for a quarter point now rather than in June. Markets had priced a hold at eighty-one per cent by Wednesday evening.

The interest is in the language. The word "gradual" has been dropped from the guidance paragraph, which the rates desk at Meridian Bank read as an opening rather than a promise.

By Tomas Enright

What a warehouse actually learns in its first year

A logistics operator in Daventry has published twelve months of floor data, unusually without a vendor's name on the cover. The headline is dull and useful: throughput rose eighteen per cent, and almost none of it came from the robots.

It came from moving the buffer zone forty metres and rewriting a shift pattern. The automation paid for itself only in the third quarter, and only on night shifts.
